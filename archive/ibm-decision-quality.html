IBM: busy analytics, unchanged decisions

A product management analysis of IBM's internal analytics program. Usage numbers look healthy. Decision behavior has barely moved. I rebuilt the metrics and the roadmap around decision quality.

Type
Individual research paper, about 20 pages
Course
INFO 7374, Northeastern University, March 2026
Frameworks
TAM/SAM/SOM, RICE, MoSCoW, North Star and KPIs, product lifecycle, stakeholder mapping, go-to-market
Sources
IBM annual results and IBM Institute for Business Value research

The question

IBM measures its internal analytics program by adoption: logins, training completions, dashboards viewed. I asked whether that tells anyone if decisions got better.

What the analysis argues

  • The program is a case of activity theater. Platform engagement is high while the way people decide has largely stayed the same.
  • Competitors such as Microsoft, Google and Salesforce put analytics inside tools people already open every day. IBM's WatsonX asks users to leave their workflow and choose to consult it.
  • The program cannot fail by its own definition, because it measures adoption rather than outcomes. That is a measurement design problem.
  • IBM's serviceable market sits in regulated industries such as financial services, healthcare, government and defense, where explainability and audit trails matter. I estimated a two to three year window to establish a validated method before competitors close it.

What I recommended

  • Make Decision Impact Rate the North Star: the share of consequential decisions where analytics changed the choice and the outcome beat the baseline.
  • Rank six initiatives with RICE. Decision logging infrastructure came first, then identifying the people who already use analytics well, then redesigning decision reviews. Extra training content ranked last.
  • Run a twelve-month roadmap in three gated phases: foundation, measurement and learning, then validation and commercial preparation.
  • Turn the internal proof into a consulting offer for regulated clients, priced in three stages from assessment to performance-linked fees. These prices are my proposals.

What I learned

The most useful move was swapping a vanity metric for an outcome metric and seeing the whole priority list reorder. The two cheapest, least glamorous items ranked highest, and the items IBM had invested in most ranked lowest.
