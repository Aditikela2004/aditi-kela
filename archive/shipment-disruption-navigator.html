Shipment disruption navigator

A concept tool that shows which inbound shipments will leave a plant short of parts, and prices each way to respond against the cost of a stopped line.

Why I built it

I built a JIT and Kanban inventory template for a supply team at Grifols, which is where safety stock and reorder timing became concrete for me. This tool asks the next question: when something is late, will we run out? Shipments and costs are synthetic.

The problem

A late shipment is not always a problem, and an on-time shipment is not always safe. What matters is whether stock lasts until it lands. Planners compare fixes such as air freight, a plant transfer or a spot buy without a shared way to price them.

What I built

  • A board of twelve shipments, sorted so the biggest problems come first.
  • A bar on each shipment comparing how long stock lasts with when it lands, with red stripes for days without parts.
  • An options screen that prices each response, marks the lowest total cost and has a slider for what if it is later.
  • A plan screen comparing the cost of doing nothing with the cost of the chosen actions.

Decisions and tradeoffs

  • Flag by stock, not by lateness. This catches the on-time shipment that arrives too late and ignores late-but-harmless ones. The tradeoff: it needs on-hand stock and daily use for every part.
  • Price the whole outcome. Each option shows the action cost plus the cost of any days still short, so a cheap fix that leaves a day short can lose to a costlier one that closes the gap.
  • Two kinds of response. An expedite moves the original shipment. A bridge adds stock while it still lands late.
  • The planner chooses. The lowest total is marked, not applied, so a person stays accountable for the spend.
  • Watch shipments mostly recommend waiting. Pricing a thin buffer needs a risk estimate I did not want to invent, and the app says so.

What I tested

  • Twenty-six unit tests, a production build and a headless-browser click-through, including the slider and the lowest-total button.

What I have not tested yet

  • No real planners, so no claims about time saved or fewer line stops.
  • Costs are placeholders, not quotes. Shipments that need the same transfer stock are not handled. The dollar figures show how the logic behaves, not real savings.

What I would do next

  • Ask three to five planners to walk the sample shipments and say which flags they disagree with.
  • Add a cost for a thin safety buffer.
  • Handle competing claims on the same stock.
  • Read transit status from a mock feed.
