At-home diagnostics: where the model breaks

Most teams tune the scheduling algorithm. I mapped the service end to end and found the binding constraint is biological, and that the growth plan is the real issue.

The question

An at-home blood draw looks simple: book, dispatch, collect, transport, process, deliver results. Where does it stop scaling?

What I found

  • Variability waste (idle time, cancellations, morning booking clusters) responds to sprint-level tuning. Structural waste (capacity committed to thin zones, long travel, spoiled samples) does not.
  • The binding constraint is the fixed biological window between collection and valid processing. Scheduling, routing and lab batching all work below that ceiling.
  • Booking works as a pull signal in dense cities. Expanding into low-density areas quietly turns it into push, because capacity is staged before anyone books.
  • Two tensions compound: one service promise over costs that vary by geography, and a consumer-speed brand running into sample viability limits.

What I recommended

  • Require a demand-density threshold before a zone gets full resource commitment, measured as completed appointments per deployed phlebotomist hour net of travel.
  • Test it with a density-gated coverage pilot: two zones, eight weeks, the same promise, with thresholds fixed before the pilot starts.
  • Track utilization net of transit, transport window adherence, sample integrity pass rate, cost per completed appointment and NPS by zone.
  • Use two-week sprints for the booking app and notifications. Do not use them for contracts and coverage promises that cannot be reversed inside a sprint.

What I learned

Whether a decision is reversible should set how fast and how formally it is made. Treating a geographic expansion like a UI change was the quiet cost in this case.
