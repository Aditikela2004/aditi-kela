Toyota: how the chip shortage missed it

When competitors idled plants in 2021, Toyota kept building. I analyzed the four systems behind that and asked whether they will hold in the next squeeze.

Individual research paper, 22 pages
Course
INFO 7374, Northeastern University, March 2026
Frameworks
SCOR, six supply chain drivers, KPI framework, stakeholder map
Sources
Toyota results and newsroom releases, S&P Global Mobility (October 2025)

The question

Toyota overtook General Motors in U.S. sales in 2021 for the first time since 1931. Analysts called it luck. I tested the opposite view: that choices made years earlier insulated it.

What I found

  • RESCUE: a multi-tier visibility database built after Fukushima that cut disruption detection from about two weeks to about 12 hours.
  • A business continuity policy that mandates two to six months of supplier buffer for critical chips.
  • A direct equity stake in the TSMC joint venture in Kumamoto, which gives Toyota a seat at the allocation table rather than a place in the queue.
  • Design for substitutability, so chips can be swapped across platforms when one source dries up.

The forward look

  • S&P Global warned in October 2025 that AI data centers are outbidding automakers for foundry capacity, with up to 600,000 fewer vehicles possible in 2026.
  • I proposed leading indicators over lagging ones: forecast compliance, buffer compliance across the tracked parts, and disruption detection time.
  • I was open about uncertainty. The second JASM phase, silicon carbide supply and the forecasting platform each carry execution risk.

What I learned

Resilience investments look unimportant until the moment they matter. Buffer inventory is a bounded recurring cost. The cost of not holding it, as other automakers found, ran into billions.
