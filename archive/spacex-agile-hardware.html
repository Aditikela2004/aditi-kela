SpaceX: agile thinking applied to rockets

I used the Agile Manifesto, Lean wastes, SCOR and TAM/SAM/SOM to explain why SpaceX gets results five to ten times better than traditional aerospace.

Course

Frameworks

Sources
NASA OIG, GAO, BryceTech, Future Market Insights and others cited in the paper

The question

NASA took thirteen years and $23.8 billion to fly the Space Launch System once. SpaceX got Starship to orbital testing in about five years for roughly $3 to 5 billion. Is that luck, money, or method?

What I found

  • Waterfall aerospace built waiting and overprocessing into its structure: freeze requirements, gate every phase, discover integration problems years later.
  • SpaceX reframed value from lift capacity to cost per kilogram, which is what customers actually buy. Falling price created customers that did not exist before.
  • Each prototype works as a sprint deliverable and a minimum viable product. Parallel prototypes acted like A/B tests for hardware. The switch from carbon fiber to stainless steel was treated as a pivot, not a program reset.
  • The biggest waste in hardware is information waste: the gap between a hypothesis and data.

Honest limits

  • Part of the gap comes from institutional differences such as congressional contract distribution and private funding. Gaps of five to ten times still point to method and organization.
  • The model leans on Starlink revenue, a flat structure that needs more structure as it grows, and a talent pipeline that is hard to scale.
  • Human-rated missions will need more process rigor, so a hybrid model is likely.

What I learned

Organizational structure is a prerequisite for a methodology, not a result of it. A competitor can copy a rocket design. Copying twenty years of reinforcing culture, supply chain and structure is much harder.
