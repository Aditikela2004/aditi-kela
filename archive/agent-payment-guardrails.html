Why I built it

At RBL Bank I wrote IT policies and controls, and a rule only felt reasonable once I saw what it did to real cases. This simulator applies that idea to AI agents that buy things: change a number, watch the outcomes move. All purchases are synthetic.

The problem

Agents are beginning to buy things for people. The owner needs to know what the agent may spend, when it must ask, and why a purchase was stopped. One on/off switch is too blunt, and approving every purchase by hand removes the point of having an agent.

What I built

  • A rules screen: limits, blocked categories, categories that always need a person, consent and fraud checks.
  • A simulated day of 14 purchase attempts, each showing how every rule voted.
  • A dashboard with approved, declined and waiting counts, daily limit used, and which rules did the most work.
  • An approval queue where a person decides held purchases, with undo.
  • A form to try a custom purchase against the current rules.

Decisions and tradeoffs

  • A decline beats a hold, and a hold beats an approval. Predictable and easy to explain. The tradeoff: a purchase that trips one hard rule never reaches a person, even if the owner might have wanted to override it.
  • Held purchases do not count toward the daily limit until approved. A queue of pending items cannot block everything else. The tradeoff: approving several later can push the day past what the owner expected.
  • Live recompute instead of a run button. Tuning thresholds is quick. Earlier approvals stay in place when a rule changes, so the reviewer uses Undo to start clean.
  • A reason for every rule. A decision without a reason is hard to trust or debug. Rows are collapsed by default to keep the table readable.

What I tested

  • Seventeen unit tests: decision order, limits at the exact boundary, invalid input, duplicate detection, purchase speed over a rolling hour, policy changes, summary totals.
  • A production build and a click-through of all three screens in a headless browser.

What I have not tested yet

  • No real users, so no claims about accuracy, fraud reduction or time saved.
  • Thresholds are placeholders and have not been compared with real spending data. The 5 / 3 / 6 split says nothing about real fraud rates.

What I would do next

  • Ask three to five people who manage payments or risk to change the rules and tell me which decisions surprise them.
  • Let the owner set rules per merchant or category.
  • Add a plain-language message when the agent is declined.
  • Store decisions in a server-side audit trail.
