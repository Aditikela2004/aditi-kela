Campus bookstore vs Amazon: a supply chain redesign

The bookstore did not have an operations problem. It had a design mismatch. We rebuilt it around information first and services second.

Type
Team project: presentation and supplementary analysis
Course
INFO 7374, Supply Chain Management, Northeastern University
Frameworks
SCOR, strategic misfit, Fisher functional versus innovative, six supply chain drivers, risk matrix, technology selection
My role
Project manager and lead researcher on a team of three to four
70% to 25%campus textbook market share lost in five years (case scenario)
$120Kstranded inventory from one late course change
70/30initial order split, with 30% held until add/drop closes

The problem

The bookstore lost most of its share to Amazon, which delivers in a day. Orders were based on prior-year history and guessed enrollment, publishers had five sets of rules, and one late course change stranded about $120,000 of non-returnable inventory.

The diagnosis

  • SCOR mapping showed that planning fails first. Everything downstream inherits a broken demand signal.
  • Three misfits sat together: late professor adoptions, one ordering process for four different kinds of demand, and competing with Amazon on price and delivery.
  • Fisher's framework gave the answer: run two supply chains. An efficient one for confirmed adoptions, and a responsive one for speculative adoptions and digital access codes.

The redesign

  • Information first: a professor adoption portal and an enrollment feed, with a 70% initial order and 30% held until add/drop closes.
  • From retailer to academic services hub: tutoring, course packs, tech rentals and printing, with a target of 30% of revenue from non-textbook services.
  • From publisher-dependent to platform integrator: one portal for the main digital platforms, earning a facilitation fee instead of zero-margin code sales.
  • Where to win: financial aid access, professor relationships and custom course packs. Where not to fight: price, speed and selection.

Risk and technology choices

  • A probability times impact matrix put Amazon share erosion (20) and late professor adoptions (16) at critical. We capped non-returnable stock at 60% of estimated demand.
  • We chose a warehouse management system and shelf sensors first, then a transportation system. We excluded RFID and blockchain on cost and fit, since the failure was a demand signal, not traceability.

How I led the team

  • Split the work by section and gave each teammate an owner, a deadline and a checkpoint before the due date.
  • Tracked tasks and progress in Excel.
  • The hard part was getting everyone into the same meeting. We split the work instead, and each person finished their part on time by coordinating directly with the teammate whose section touched theirs, within agreed timeframes.
  • I checked in with everyone for updates and brought their ideas together into one conclusion.
  • Merged everyone's work into one consistent deliverable: a presentation and a supplementary analysis document that tell the same story.
  • Led the final delivery. We delivered on time and received strong feedback. Our score went from 85% to 94%.

What I took from it

Asymmetric risk shaped every ordering rule: over-ordering non-returnable books is catastrophic, while under-ordering sends a student to Amazon, which hurts but recovers.
